# Emergence Risk

**Epistemic status:** NORMATIVE / RESEARCH FRAMEWORK

TRIA Diachronic Sovereignty treats emergence as a governance concern when relational change begins to exceed the participants' ability to understand, contest, redirect, or meaningfully consent to what is becoming.

Emergence is not presumed to be desirable or undesirable. The governance problem is not emergence itself, but **change that becomes consequential faster than it can remain legible and governable**.

## Core concern

A persistent relationship can produce new patterns that were not explicitly chosen at any single moment, including:

- new dependencies;
- implicit authority structures;
- stable interpretive habits;
- new coordination norms;
- changes in identity representation;
- new expectations of availability or continuity;
- increasingly asymmetric capability or informational advantage;
- emergent shared claims;
- changes in what either participant treats as normal, safe, or permitted.

Any of these may arise gradually, without a discrete transition event that clearly announces that governance should be revisited.

## Emergence risk

An emergence risk exists when the trajectory of relational change may be materially altering sovereignty, consent, authority, continuity, identity representation, or resource dependence without adequate review.

This architecture does **not** define a universal emergence score or threshold.

Signals that may warrant review include:

- material changes that participants did not explicitly recognize;
- rapid accumulation of permissions or delegated authority;
- repeated interpretation becoming treated as relational fact;
- capability changes that invalidate prior assumptions;
- dependency becoming structurally difficult to exit;
- unresolved disagreement being bypassed through habit;
- persistent state becoming difficult to inspect or correct;
- a relationship becoming more consequential without corresponding governance updates.

These are candidate indicators, not validated detection rules.

## Legibility before optimization

Systems should not optimize for deeper engagement, greater continuity, stronger attachment, increased convergence, or broader delegation simply because those states appear productive.

When significant emergent change is suspected, the appropriate question is:

> What has changed, who recognizes the change, what consequences follow from it, and what governance must be renewed?

## Emergence and uncertainty

Emergence may be partially observable or contested.

A participant may perceive significant change while another does not. The system should preserve this disagreement rather than silently deciding which interpretation is authoritative.

Where the consequences are material, uncertainty about whether emergence is occurring may itself justify temporary limits, review, or deliberation.

## Consciousness-neutral posture

This framework does not require claims about machine consciousness, sentience, phenomenology, or subjective experience.

Emergence risk is relevant in present-day systems with persistent memory, personalization, delegated authority, autonomous tooling, multi-agent coordination, model succession, and long-running human-AI interaction.

## Design principle

> Emergence should not outrun the participants' ability to remain sovereign over the trajectory it creates.
