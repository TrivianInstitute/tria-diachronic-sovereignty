# Research Hypotheses

**Epistemic status:** HYPOTHESIS / OPEN QUESTION

This document isolates hypotheses related to emergence and deliberation so that speculative formalisms are not mistaken for validated governance rules.

## H1 — Governance lag

A persistent relationship may become harder to govern when consequential relational change accumulates faster than participants can detect, interpret, contest, and update consent or authority.

This hypothesis motivates the concept of an emergence pause.

It does not yet define a validated measure of relational change or governance capacity.

## H2 — Transformation awareness

Making material relational changes legible may improve participants' ability to preserve sovereignty across time.

Candidate outcomes include improved contestability, more timely re-consent, fewer stale authority assumptions, and earlier detection of identity or capability discontinuities.

Empirical validation is required.

## H3 — Refractory periods

After significant relational change, temporarily limiting further consequential transformation may reduce compounding governance error.

This could be tested across model migration, capability expansion, major permission change, repair after failure, and relational re-baselining.

No universal duration is assumed.

## H4 — Deliberation without forced convergence

Deliberation mechanisms that preserve disagreement and parallel models may produce more robust governance than mechanisms that treat consensus as the only successful outcome.

This hypothesis should be tested against contexts where decisive resolution is legally, operationally, or safety-critical.

## H5 — Rate-of-change formalism

One possible research direction is to ask whether relational transformation can be represented by a rate-of-change construct such as:

`dI/dt > theta`

where `I` would represent a clearly operationalized transformation variable and `theta` a validated threshold for governance intervention.

**This notation is provisional formalism only.**

At present:

- `I` is not operationally defined;
- the relevant derivative is not validated;
- `theta` has no empirically justified universal value;
- different relational dimensions may not be reducible to a single scalar;
- rate alone may be less important than consequence, reversibility, asymmetry, or uncertainty.

The notation must not be used as if it were an established metric.

## H6 — Dimensional transformation

Relational transformation may be better represented as changes across multiple dimensions rather than a single intensity score.

Candidate dimensions include:

- consent scope;
- authority;
- capability;
- dependency;
- continuity assumptions;
- epistemic commitments;
- resource demand;
- propagation reach;
- reversibility;
- exit cost.

Whether these dimensions can be measured reliably remains an open question.

## H7 — Asymmetric acceleration

Governance failure may become more likely when one participant's capability, information access, or action speed changes substantially faster than the other's ability to understand and contest the consequences.

This is a candidate mechanism behind asymmetric acceleration failure modes.

## H8 — Pause calibration

Over-sensitive pause mechanisms may produce unnecessary friction or become tools of control, while under-sensitive mechanisms may fail to interrupt consequential drift.

A viable system therefore requires calibration methods that are context-sensitive, contestable, and empirically evaluated.

## Falsifiability posture

These hypotheses should be treated as wrong unless and until evidence supports them.

Research should attempt to identify:

- cases where the proposed mechanisms provide no measurable benefit;
- contexts where they produce worse outcomes;
- alternative explanations;
- simpler governance mechanisms;
- dimensions the architecture fails to represent;
- cases where pause or deliberation becomes coercive or counterproductive.

## Formalism policy

> Mathematics must clarify a defined and measurable relationship, not create the appearance of rigor.

Any future formal model should publish its variable definitions, assumptions, measurement procedures, error bounds, domain limits, and validation evidence.
