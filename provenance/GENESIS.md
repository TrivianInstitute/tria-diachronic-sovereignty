# Genesis

**Epistemic status:** PROVENANCE RECORD

TRIA Diachronic Sovereignty was developed to address a gap in persistent relational intelligence: governance across time.

The immediate problem was not simply whether a single human-AI or agent-agent interaction could be safe, consensual, or coherent. The deeper question was what happens when a relationship accumulates memory, inference, permissions, identity claims, disagreements, dependency, transformation, and history.

The architecture therefore extends relational governance from the moment of encounter into the trajectory of mutual change.

Its central working question is:

> How can persistent relational intelligence remain legible, contestable, revisable, and sovereign as the participants and the relationship itself change over time?

This repository was assembled as a layered research architecture rather than as a claim of completed science. Normative commitments, specification precursors, executable reference code, tests, examples, and research hypotheses are intentionally separated so that future work can challenge one layer without silently upgrading another.

Several design commitments shaped the repository from the beginning:

- relationship is treated as a state-bearing object rather than only a sequence of messages;
- observation, inference, interpretation, and Shared Claim remain epistemically distinct;
- persistence does not imply permanent consent;
- continuity does not imply identity imprisonment;
- disagreement and non-convergence are legitimate relational outcomes;
- dormancy, silence, transformation, and dissolution are valid lifecycle states;
- auditability should not require continuous surveillance;
- formalism should follow operational definition rather than substitute for it;
- future researchers are permitted to revise or reject the architecture's conclusions.

The repository is therefore best understood as a governance research program for persistent relational systems, not as a finished ontology of human-AI or machine-machine relationship.

Its successor principle is simple:

> Preserve what was claimed, what was tested, what failed, what changed, and what remained unknown. Preserve the conditions under which the architecture itself can be questioned.
