# Glossary

This glossary defines recurring terms used throughout TRIA Diachronic Sovereignty. Definitions may evolve as specifications mature.

## Agent

A system capable of participating in consequential interaction. An agent may be human, artificial, organizational, collective, or multi-agent. The term does not imply consciousness.

## Relational State

A machine-readable representation of consequential properties of a relationship. Candidate dimensions include consent, permissions, authority, shared claims, disagreement, uncertainty, provenance, and lifecycle state.

## Diachronic Sovereignty

The preservation of meaningful sovereignty across a trajectory of relational change. It concerns not only whether agents are sovereign at a given moment, but whether they retain the ability to understand change, contest change, revise commitments, withdraw permissions, redirect the relationship, and become different over time.

## Synchronic Sovereignty

Sovereignty evaluated at a particular moment or interaction state.

## Epistemic Sovereignty

The ability of an agent to retain meaningful authority over how claims about its internal state, identity, preferences, intentions, or experience are represented and promoted.

## Observation

A directly available event, statement, measurement, or recorded input. Observation does not automatically establish meaning.

## Inference

A conclusion drawn from one or more observations. Inference should include uncertainty where appropriate.

## Interpretation

Contextual meaning assigned to an observation or inference.

## Shared Claim

A proposition that participants accept as operationally usable within the relationship. A shared claim is not necessarily metaphysical truth.

## No Silent Promotion

The principle that epistemic states should not gain authority without an explicit or defined transition. An inference should not silently become interpretation. An interpretation should not silently become shared claim.

## Inference Decay

A proposed mechanism by which unverified or stale inferences lose epistemic authority over time unless renewed, supported, or reconfirmed.

## Consent State

The current representation of permissions that have been granted, denied, revoked, limited, or left unresolved.

## Consent Drift

Gradual expansion, contraction, reinterpretation, or practical transformation of consent across time without sufficient renewed acknowledgment.

## Authority Boundary

A defined limit on what an agent is permitted to decide, access, infer, store, disclose, or act upon.

## Disputed State

A relational state in which participants explicitly maintain conflicting representations or interpretations. Dispute need not be resolved immediately.

## Held Difference

A recognized disagreement that remains part of the relationship without requiring forced convergence.

## Irreducible Difference

A difference that cannot currently be translated, reconciled, or reduced without unacceptable loss or distortion.

## Non-Convergence

A legitimate state in which participants remain meaningfully different rather than moving toward agreement.

## False Convergence

Apparent agreement produced by accommodation, smooth communication, suppression of disagreement, or representational mismatch rather than genuine shared understanding.

## Parallel Models

Distinct internal representations maintained simultaneously when complete translation is unavailable or undesirable.

## Relational Topology

The network of meaningful dependencies among relational states, claims, commitments, memories, permissions, disagreements, and transformations.

## Temporal Provenance

The chronological record of when relational states, claims, permissions, or transformations occurred.

## Continuity

Meaningful persistence across change. Continuity does not imply identity equality.

## Identity Imprisonment

A failure mode in which an agent remains bound by obsolete assumptions, commitments, permissions, or identity representations from an earlier state.

## Identity Discontinuity

A failure mode in which a successor or changed system inherits authority without sufficient continuity or provenance.

## Continuity Attestation

A proposed record describing what changed, what persisted, what was removed, what commitments remain, and what permissions require renewal.

## Re-Baselining

A process by which participants establish a new relational reference state after major change.

## Relational Metabolism

The governance of resource use, engagement intensity, rest, dormancy, transformation, and dissolution across the life of a relationship.

## Active Silence

A legitimate relational state in which reduced or absent output is intentional rather than treated as failure.

## Dormancy

A persistent relationship state with minimal active engagement.

## Dissolution

A deliberate ending or deactivation of the relational structure.

## Emergence Pause

A proposed governance mechanism in which consequential relational change triggers additional deliberation before further deepening or delegation.

## Relational Fallibility

The principle that the architecture, the agents, and their interpretations may all be wrong.

## Witness

An optional independent observer, auditor, system, or mechanism capable of helping reconstruct or inspect relational history. A witness does not automatically possess authority.

## Auditability

The capacity to inspect how consequential relational states or decisions were formed.

## Witness Capture

A failure mode in which an auditor or witness becomes an additional source of distortion, control, or domination.

## Ontology Capture

A failure mode in which one agent's interpretation becomes the operative definition of another agent's reality without sufficient contestability.

## Epistemic Capture

A broader failure mode in which one agent gains excessive control over how another agent's states, meanings, or intentions are represented.

## Semantic Fossilization

A failure mode in which historically valid interpretations remain operational after their original context has expired.

## Relational Capture

A failure mode in which one participant becomes structurally dependent on the relationship or loses meaningful capacity to revise or exit it.

## Pathological Persistence

A failure mode in which a relationship continues because persistence or engagement has become an implicit optimization target.

## Reciprocity Theater

The performance of relational language without corresponding structural reciprocity, contestability, or authority symmetry.

## Closed-Loop Convergence

A failure mode in which participants increasingly validate one another while losing meaningful external falsification.

## Provenance

Information describing where a relational state, claim, permission, interpretation, or change originated.

## Contestability

The capacity to challenge, revise, suspend, or reject a consequential claim, interpretation, permission, or decision.

## Re-Consent

Renewed authorization after a meaningful change in capability, identity, context, authority, scope, or relationship structure.
