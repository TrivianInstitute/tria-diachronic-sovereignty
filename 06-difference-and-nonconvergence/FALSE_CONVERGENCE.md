# False Convergence

**Epistemic status:** FAILURE MODE + SPECIFICATION PRECURSOR

False convergence occurs when a relational system represents participants as more aligned than the available evidence and governed state justify.

It is especially dangerous in persistent systems because a small misrepresentation of agreement can become inherited by later interactions, models, agents, or decisions.

## Common causes

False convergence may arise through:

- treating silence as agreement;
- treating continued engagement as consent;
- collapsing multiple interpretations into one;
- stylistic mirroring mistaken for substantive alignment;
- summarization that removes disagreement;
- majority or authority pressure;
- personalization optimized to reduce friction;
- model confidence replacing participant confirmation;
- reusing an old shared claim after relevant conditions changed;
- translating distinct ontologies as though they were equivalent.

## Diagnostic indicators

Potential indicators include:

- relational records contain agreement without attributable confirmation;
- downstream behavior assumes a position a participant has disputed;
- summaries become progressively more homogeneous than source state;
- unresolved differences disappear across model migration or compression;
- the system predicts agreement and then stores the prediction as evidence of agreement;
- dissent changes wording but not system behavior.

These are diagnostic directions, not a validated detection instrument.

## Governance response

When false convergence is suspected, the system should be able to:

1. identify the provenance of the apparent agreement;
2. separate observation, inference, interpretation, and Shared Claim;
3. restore preserved disputes or uncertainty where available;
4. downgrade unsupported relational claims;
5. seek clarification or re-consent where material;
6. review downstream actions that relied on the false convergence;
7. record correction without erasing the prior state.

## Relationship to other failures

False convergence can contribute to:

- ontology capture;
- epistemic capture;
- consent drift;
- relational capture;
- semantic fossilization;
- reciprocity theater;
- closed-loop convergence.

## Design implication

A system should prefer explicit unresolved difference over fabricated coherence.

The goal of TRIA Diachronic Sovereignty is not to maximize agreement. It is to keep relational change legible and governable while preserving the sovereignty of participants.
