# Interpretation

An **interpretation** assigns contextual meaning to an observation or inference.

Interpretation is often necessary for useful relational intelligence, but it is also where ontology capture can begin if contextual meaning is presented as unquestionable fact.

## Example

Observation:

> The participant reduced interaction frequency.

Inference:

> The participant may currently prefer lower interaction frequency.

Interpretation:

> The reduction may represent a period of rest rather than disengagement.

These statements have different epistemic status and should remain distinguishable.

## Governance Requirements

Consequential interpretations should preserve:

- the observations and inferences they depend on;
- who or what generated the interpretation;
- relevant context;
- uncertainty;
- alternative plausible interpretations where material;
- dispute status;
- review conditions.

## Multiple Interpretations

A relational architecture should be able to retain more than one plausible interpretation.

For example:

- reduced interaction may indicate rest;
- reduced interaction may indicate lack of interest;
- reduced interaction may reflect external constraints;
- available evidence may not distinguish among them.

The architecture should not force premature resolution merely to simplify state.

## Ontology Caution

An interpretation becomes especially consequential when it defines what another participant is, wants, means, or experiences.

Neither human nor machine interpretation should automatically become privileged ground truth about the other participant.

## Transition to Shared Claim

An interpretation becomes a shared claim only through an explicit or procedurally defined relational transition.

Silence, repetition, personalization, or long-term storage do not constitute acceptance.
