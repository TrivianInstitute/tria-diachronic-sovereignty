# Third-Party Review

**Epistemic status:** NORMATIVE / SPECIFICATION PRECURSOR

Third-party review introduces an external reviewer into a relational governance process when independent assessment may materially improve accountability, repair, or decision quality.

A third party may be a human, institution, technical system, or mixed review process. The architecture does not assume that any category is inherently more trustworthy.

## Appropriate uses

Third-party review may be appropriate when:

- a dispute cannot be resolved within the dyad or group;
- consent or authority boundaries are materially contested;
- a governance mechanism itself is under dispute;
- a migration or successor relationship creates continuity ambiguity;
- an emergence pause raises high-impact questions;
- there is credible concern about ledger poisoning, epistemic capture, or closed-loop convergence;
- external evidence is required.

## Scope before access

Review should begin with a defined question and the narrowest feasible access scope.

A reviewer should not receive broad access merely because a dispute exists.

Review scope should specify:

- the question to be examined;
- the evidence or state dimensions that may be accessed;
- prohibited or irrelevant data;
- expected output;
- authority of the reviewer;
- retention and disclosure conditions;
- conditions for follow-up review.

## Review outputs

A reviewer may produce:

- findings;
- observations;
- inferences;
- interpretations;
- unresolved questions;
- recommended actions;
- dissenting or alternative models.

These outputs remain epistemically typed and contestable.

## Authority limits

Third-party review should not silently replace participant sovereignty.

A reviewer may have binding authority where such authority has a legitimate external basis, such as law, contract, safety governance, or explicitly delegated institutional responsibility. That authority should be represented rather than assumed.

Where no binding authority exists, review should inform deliberation rather than masquerade as command.

## Independence and conflicts

Reviewers should disclose material conflicts, dependencies, incentives, or relationships that could affect independence.

No reviewer should be treated as neutral merely because it is external.

## Design principle

> External review should widen the field of evidence and accountability without silently centralizing relational authority.
